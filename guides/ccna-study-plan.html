A week-by-week CCNA study plan for complete beginners

By Ata Tunc Bilge · Published 1 October 2026

This plan is for people starting the CCNA 200-301 with little or no networking background. It assumes about 72 hours of focused study, the middle of a realistic 60 to 80 hour range for a beginner, and shows how that time fits into 20, 30 or 45 minutes a day. Focused means actively learning: reading or watching a lesson, answering questions, typing commands, drilling subnetting and reviewing mistakes. A video playing in the background does not count.

The topic order follows the six domains of the CCNA 200-301 v1.1 blueprint, which applies to exams taken up to and including 2 February 2027. If your exam will be later than that, read the section on exam dates near the end.

IP Connectivity gets the most time because it carries the highest weight (25%) and because routing-table and OSPF questions reward practice. Foundations is not an exam domain, but without it subnetting and switching are much harder to follow. The last 9 hours are for review and full mock exams, not new material.

Once a week, replace new material with a review session: redo the questions you got wrong that week and write one line for each explaining why the right answer is right.

Rules that keep beginners on track

  1. Start subnetting early and keep it going. Subnetting appears in addressing, routing, ACL and troubleshooting questions. After the Network Fundamentals block, drill it two or three times a week until exam day. Keep the subnetting cheat sheet as a reference, not a crutch.
  2. Type the commands. Reading show ip route output is not the same as producing it. Use a simulator such as Cisco Packet Tracer (free through Cisco Networking Academy), lab equipment or command labs, and type every configuration you study.
  3. Review mistakes, not scores. A question you got right by guessing teaches nothing. Keep a short error log and revisit it every week.
  4. Use spaced repetition for facts. Port numbers, administrative distances, IPv6 address types and wireless standards stick better with flashcards than with rereading.
  5. Measure readiness with unseen questions. Scores on questions you have already answered overstate what you know. Judge readiness on first-try answers and on timed mocks.
  6. Pick an exam date after Network Fundamentals. By then you know your real pace. Booking on day one adds pressure; never booking lets the plan drift.

How to know you are ready

Cisco does not publish the passing score for the CCNA, so there is no official percentage to aim for. Leave a margin: look for consistently strong results on timed mock exams you have not seen before, across every domain, rather than one good score in your favorite topics. Before booking, you should be able to:

  • Subnet any IPv4 address and prefix length in well under a minute, without a calculator.
  • Explain why a switch port is forwarding or blocking, and why a route is in the routing table.
  • Configure VLANs, trunks, static routes, single-area OSPF, NAT and standard and extended ACLs from memory.
  • Score consistently well on full-length timed mocks, with no domain far below the others.

As one example of a conservative target, Linkwing assumes that about 80% correct corresponds to roughly 825 out of 1000. Treat any such figure as an estimate.

Your exam date decides the version

Exams taken up to and including 2 February 2027 use CCNA 200-301 v1.1; exams from 3 February 2027 use v2.0, which reorganizes the domains and changes some topics. Starting from zero on 1 October 2026 and studying every day, this 72-hour plan finishes around:
